Matrix 常用技巧！！

1. Finding Inverse ($A^{-1}$) From Polynomials

When To Use
  • $A$ is unknown, so you cannot directly compute $A^{-1}$.
  • You have a polynomial equation in terms of $A$.

Skill 1.1

If you are given a polynomial equation in terms of $A$ and asked to find $A^{-1}$:


  1. Isolate the identity matrix $I$ on one side of the equation.
  2. Factor out the matrix $A$.
  3. Divide both sides by the coefficient of $I$.
  4. Use the definition of inverse: $AB=BA=I \implies B=A^{-1}$

Example 1.1.1

Given $A^3 + A^2 + A + 2I = 0$, find $A^{-1}$.


Solution

  1. Isolate the identity matrix $I$ on one side of the equation: $$-2I = A^3 + A^2 + A$$
  2. Factor out the matrix $A$: $$-2I = A(A^2 + A + I)$$
  3. Divide both sides by the coefficient of $I$: $$I = A[-\dfrac{1}{2}(A^2 + A + I)]$$
  4. Since $A \textcolor{red}{[-\dfrac{1}{2}(A^2 + A + I)]} = \textcolor{red}{[-\dfrac{1}{2}(A^2 + A + I)]}A = I$, we have $$A^{-1} = \textcolor{red}{[-\dfrac{1}{2}(A^2 + A + I)]}$$

Hint

留意下低嘅題目，左邊都係一個 polynomial!

2. Diagonalization ($A=PDP^{-1}$)

When To Use
  • You need to find $A^n$ for a large integer $n$.
  • You have found a matrix $P$ such that $P^{-1}AP$ equals a diagonal matrix $D$.

Skill 2.1

If you are given / calculated $P^{-1}AP=D$ (or $PAP^{-1}=D$，是但啦) and you need to find $A^n$:


  1. Apply the power on both sides: $P^{-1}A^{\textcolor{red}{n}}P=D^{\textcolor{red}{n}}$.
  2. Calculate $D^n$: $D^n = \begin{pmatrix} a^n & 0 \\ 0 & b^n \end{pmatrix}$.
  3. Multiply on the left by $P$ and on the right by $P^{-1}$ to get $A^n = PD^nP^{-1}$.
  4. Compute $PD^nP^{-1}$.

Example 2.1.1

Given $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Find $P^{-1}AP$. Hence, find $A^n$, where $n$ is a positive integer.


Solution

  1. Find $P^{-1}$:
    $$P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3} \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$
  2. Compute $P^{-1}AP$:
    $$P^{-1}AP = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$$
    $$= \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
  3. Apply the $n$-th power on both sides:
    $$P^{-1}A^nP=\begin{pmatrix} 5^n & 0 \\ 0 & 2^n \end{pmatrix}$$
  4. Rearrange:
    $$A^n = P\begin{pmatrix} 5^n & 0 \\ 0 & 2^n \end{pmatrix}P^{-1}$$
  5. Compute $A^n$:
    $$A^n = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5^n & 0 \\ 0 & 2^n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} $$
    $$= \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 5^n + 2^n & 5^n - 2^n \\ 2 \cdot 5^n - 2^{n+1} & 5^n + 2^{n+1} \end{pmatrix}$$

Hint
  • 幾乎所有 $P^{-1}AP$ 最終得出嘅都係一個 diagonal matrix，如果計唔到就再驗算一次！
  • 最終可以代 $n=1$ check R.H.S. 等唔等於 L.H.S.
    e.g. 上面 $A^1 = \dfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = A$

Summation of Diagonalizable Matrices

Skill 2.2

  • If the question is a sum of diagonalizable matrices ($PXP^{-1}$), consider factoring out $P$ and $P^{-1}$ for the whole sum.
    i.e. $P(X_1+X_2+ \dots + X_n)P^{-1}$
  • Note that $I$ can be written as $PIP^{-1}$, it is also diagonalizable!
  • See if you can apply the geometric sum in the middle part:
    $$a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n = \dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}$$

Example 2.2.1

Given $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ such that $P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Find $I+A+A^2+A^3+ \dots +A^{10}$.


Solution

$I+A+A^2+A^3+ \dots +A^{10}$

$=PIP^{-1}+PDP^{-1} + PD^2P^{-1} + PD^3P^{-1} + \dots + PD^{10}P^{-1}$

$=P \left( I+D + D^2 + D^3 + \dots + D^{10} \right) P^{-1}$

$=P \begin{pmatrix} 1+6+6^2+\dots+6^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 1+1^1+1^2+\dots+1^{10} \end{pmatrix} P^{-1}$

$=P \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{6^{11}-1}{6-1} & 0 \\ 0 & 11 \end{pmatrix} P^{-1}$

$=\dfrac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{6^{11}-1}{5} & 0 \\ 0 & 11 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$

$= \begin{pmatrix} \frac{4(6^{11}) + 51}{25} & \frac{2(6^{11}) - 112}{25} \\ \frac{2(6^{11}) - 112}{25} & \frac{6^{11} + 219}{25} \end{pmatrix}$

3. Binomial Theorem

Skill 3.1 —— Binomial Theorem

If (and only if) $AB=BA$, then $(A+B)^n$ can be expanded by using the binomial theorem.

Usually, there are two directions to use this theorem:

Direction 1: Prove $(pA+qB)^n = p^nA+q^nB$

Skill 3.2.1 —— Idempotent Matrix

If $A^2=A$, then $A^n=A$. (Cannot directly use, need proof)


Proof:

$A^n = A^2 \cdot A^{n-2} = A \cdot A^{n-2} = A^{n-1}$

$\therefore A^n = A^{n-1} = A^{n-2} = \dots = A^2 = A$


(A square matrix is idempotent if $A^2=A$.)

Skill 3.2.2 —— $(pA+qB)^n = p^nA+q^nB$

Let $p$ and $q$ be real numbers. If:

  • $A^2=A$ and $B^2=B$ (so $A^n=A$ and $B^n=B$)
  • $AB=BA=\mathbf{0}$ (so middle terms vanishes)

Then $(pA+qB)^n = p^nA+q^nB$. (Cannot directly use, need proof)


Proof:

$(pA+qB)^n$

$=p^n A^n + \textcolor{red}{C^n_1 p^{n-1}A^{n-1}qB + C^n_2 p^{n-2}A^{n-2}q^2 B^2 + \dots + C^n_{n-1} pAq^{n-1}B^{n-1}} + q^nB^n$
(Note that each term in red has at least one $AB$)

$=p^nA^n + q^nB^n$ (By using $AB=BA=\mathbf{0}$)

$=p^nA+q^nB$ (By using $A^n=A$ and $B^n=B$)

Example 3.2.2.1

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$.

(a) Find $A^2$, $B^2$, $AB$ and $BA$.

(b) Using the result from (a), evaluate $\begin{pmatrix} 8 & -3 \\ 14 & -5 \end{pmatrix}^{10}$.


Solution

(a)

$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix} =A$

$B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix} = B$

$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}$

$BA = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 3 \\ -14 & 7 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ 14 & -6 \end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}$


(b)

Notice that $\begin{pmatrix} 8 & -3 \\ 14 & -5 \end{pmatrix} = 2A+B$.

Since $AB=BA=0$, we can expand $(2A+B)^{10}$ by using the binomial theorem:

$\begin{pmatrix} 8 & -3 \\ 14 & -5 \end{pmatrix}^{10} = (2A + B)^{10}$

$=2^{10}A^{10}+\textcolor{blue}{C_1^{10} 2^9 A^9 B + C_2^{10} 2^8 A^8 B^2 + \dots + C_{n-1}^{10} 2AB^9}+B^10$

$=2^{10}A^{10}+\mathbf{0}+B^{10}$

$=2^{10}A^{10}+B^{10}$


$A^{n} = A^2A^{n-2} = AA^{n-2} = A^{n-1}$, where n is a postive integer.

Therefore, $\textcolor{blue}{A^{10}=A^9=A^8=\dots=A^2=A}$.

Similarly, we have $B^{10}=B$.

$\therefore 2^{10}A^{10}+B^{10}$

$=1024A+B$

$= \begin{pmatrix} 7162 & -3069 \\ 14322 & -6137 \end{pmatrix}$
